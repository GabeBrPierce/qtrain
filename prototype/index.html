What the research says

The claim. Princeton's PEAR lab (1979 to 2007) and others reported that people could nudge hardware random number generators by intention, by tiny amounts (around 1 extra hit per 10,000).

The meta-analysis. Bösch, Steinkamp & Boller (Psychological Bulletin, 2006) pooled 380 RNG studies. They found a minuscule effect, with the smallest studies showing the biggest effects, a pattern typical of publication bias, and concluded the result could be explained by it.

Replication. A large preregistered study by Maier et al. (Frontiers in Psychology, 2018) found Bayesian evidence against intention effects on quantum randomness. PEAR's own multi-lab replication did not reproduce its earlier results.

About this source. The lizaonair service measures one quantum seed from ANU's vacuum-fluctuation lab every ~8 hours, then expands it with HMAC-SHA256. So individual numbers are not separate quantum events: between seed refreshes, they are fixed by a deterministic algorithm and a counter shared with every other user. Even if mind-over-matter were real, there is no quantum measurement happening when you press the button.

What this means for odds. No skill has been shown to beat a properly random source. Casinos, lotteries and online games use the same kind of generator, so practice here will not shift odds there. Use this app as an honest test: set a session length before you start, finish it, and read the verdict. Stopping when you are ahead is the most common way people fool themselves.
